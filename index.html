Overview

Sensorimotor coordination in long daily tasks

Abstract

Existing human-activity datasets typically cover only one or two sensor channels and often focus on short, isolated actions, leaving long-duration compositional tasks largely unsupported as benchmarks for temporal cross-modal sensorimotor learning. We introduce PULSE (Physiological Unified Long-duration Synchronized Embodiment), a synchronized five-modality dataset that enables benchmarking temporal cross-modal sensorimotor learning: how models recognize ongoing activities, anticipate future contact, reconstruct unobserved signals, and remain robust when physiological, motion, gaze, inertial, and tactile channels are partially observed. Collected from 40 volunteers across 8 ecologically valid daily-activity scenarios together with a dedicated motion-primitive collection, PULSE records full-body optical motion capture with finger-level hand articulation, surface EMG, binocular eye tracking, wearable IMU, and a finger-level pressure array recording quantitative grip force, all sampled at 100 Hz. Each scenario is a long-horizon compositional task containing many smaller sub-tasks, yielding over 7,300 annotated action segments. Each segment is tagged with a motor primitive, the hand involved, the manipulated object, and a natural-language description with four paraphrased variants.

On top of the dataset, we define multiple benchmark tasks for temporal cross-modal sensorimotor learning: scene and fine-grained action recognition, grasp onset anticipation, missing-modality robustness, tactile-driven sub-second grasp-state prediction, cross-modal pressure reconstruction, etc. Together these tasks evaluate how information transfers across physiology, motion, gaze, and touch, rather than reducing the dataset to an activity-label catalog. We evaluate three backbone architectures, nine fusion strategies, seven published baselines, and task-specific models including SyncFuse and DailyActFormer. Detailed task definitions and results are in the Appendices. Data, annotations, and baseline code will be publicly released.

The dataset, its annotations and the baseline code are already available on Hugging Face; see Data format and download.

Why the streams must share one clock

  1. Gaze leads the hand. In activities of daily living the eyes fixate the next object of interest well before the hand arrives (Land et al., 1999; Hayhoe & Ballard, 2005).
  2. Muscles fire before force. Grip force is scaled in a feed-forward manner, with EMG activation preceding the grasping force by hundreds of milliseconds (Johansson & Westling, 1984). This coupling is only observable when EMG is recorded synchronously with kinematics and contact.
  3. Touch is needed for dexterity. Removing cutaneous force feedback degrades precision-grip force control even when vision and proprioception remain intact (Monzée et al., 2003).

Existing activity datasets instrument only a slice of this loop: skeleton datasets discard physiological signals, hand–object datasets omit gaze and EMG, and contact data, when present, is limited to thermal maps or binary tactile gloves. PULSE is positioned as a high-density, hardware-synchronized benchmark rather than a large-scale activity corpus.

Five modalities on one 100 Hz time base

All five sensor streams are synchronized to a unified 100 Hz time base. A shared trigger from the Qualisys motion-capture controller starts the other acquisition systems at the beginning of each trial, and residual clock drift is corrected in post-processing by cross-correlating the inertial and motion-capture signals. Each device records at its own native rate; the release resamples every stream onto the shared 100 Hz grid, so for slower devices the grid values are interpolated. Eight long-horizon daily-activity scenarios

The scenarios span work (office, shipping, packing) and household life (kitchen, dining cleanup and setup, beverage, clothes). Before each recording, volunteers heard a short verbal description of the target scenario; beyond this high-level goal they completed the task in their own style and at their own pace. Each recording is one complete execution of a scenario and chains dozens of sub-actions.

Two-level action annotations

L1 Scenario
The high-level goal of a recording, one of 8 classes (for example, post-meal table cleaning).
L2 Action segment
A dense temporal segment with a motor primitive from an 18-class vocabulary, the acting hand (left, right or both), the primary manipulated object from a scenario-specific whitelist, and a natural-language description with four text variants.

282 of the 304 task recordings, from 36 volunteers, carry dense segment annotations: 7,356 segments, about 26 per recording. The two levels support goal-conditioned action segmentation, sub-task prediction, hierarchical imitation learning and procedure-level evaluation.

How segments are produced

Segments are generated by a VLM-assisted annotation pipeline. For each recording, compressed first-person eye-tracker video is passed to a vision-language model (Gemini 3 Pro) under a scene-specific structured prompt that injects the fixed 18-primitive vocabulary and a per-scene object whitelist, constraining the output to a closed vocabulary.

Every annotated recording was then reviewed by a human segment by segment, and each segment was confirmed or corrected, so all released segments have been checked and corrected by a human.

Boundaries have 1 s resolution and describe semantic action intervals, not 100 Hz event timestamps. Sub-second benchmark labels are derived from the synchronized sensor streams.

Benchmarks as multi-axis generalization probes

Rather than reducing the dataset to a list of activity-recognition tasks, the benchmark identifies axes along which training and deployment conditions can diverge and probes each one: a case study on anticipatory coordination (F0) and five generalization probes (F1–F5) instantiated as tasks T1–T7. Every task uses volunteer-stratified 5-fold cross-validation with disjoint training and test volunteers, so all numbers measure generalization to unseen people. Inputs are anti-alias downsampled from 100 Hz to 20 Hz.

F0 · Case study

Muscle activity and hand motion lead contact by about half a second

Grasp onset is defined as the moment total hand pressure crosses 5 g from below. Of 3,723 grasp events detected across 80 recordings, 413 clean events that start from a quiescent pre-grasp period are retained. Aligned to contact and averaged over these events, the EMG envelope and hand velocity rise well before the pressure jump (first figure). Per-event onset estimates average −568 ± 370 ms for EMG (median −530 ms) and −586 ± 355 ms for hand motion (median −580 ms) relative to contact (second figure).

Reference models

SyncFuse T1

A late-fusion model built for PULSE. Each of the five modalities passes through its own two-layer MLP → BiGRU stem with LayerNorm; per-frame features are concatenated across modalities, passed through a two-layer BiLSTM and attention-pooled over time. Per-sample modality dropout (p = 0.3) during training enforces graceful degradation. 1.6M parameters.

Macro F1 0.626 ± 0.099 on T1, +5.6 pp over the strongest published baseline (CTR-GCN).

DailyActFormer T2

A multi-modal action classifier built on HandFormer's multi-scale convolutional backbone, with per-modality multi-scale 1-D conv stems, modality dropout (p = 0.3), concat → 1×1-conv fusion and attention pooling over time, feeding a joint verb–noun head.

act_vn@5 0.403 ± 0.029 on T2. Margins over the strongest published baselines are small and confidence intervals overlap, so it is released as a strong baseline rather than a decisive model contribution.

Baseline suite

Three backbones (CNN1D, BiLSTM, Transformer) and nine fusion strategies (early, late, weighted late, gated late, attention, stacking, product of experts, mixture of experts, feature concatenation).

Published methods. T1: ST-GCN, CTR-GCN, LIMU-BERT, an sEMG CNN, ActionSense LSTM, MulT and Perceiver IO, reproduced with their original architectures. T2: DeepConvLSTM, RU-LSTM, FUTR, AFFT and HandFormer, with architectures and hyperparameters adapted from each paper's recommendation, plus a from-scratch ActionLLM surrogate. T6–T7: Pham BiLSTM, Ghorbani GRU, EMG2Pose and NeuroPose.

Across tasks no single modality dominates: EMG is the strongest single input for grasp anticipation (T3), hand MoCap for spatial pressure regression (T6b), and MoCap and IMU carry most of the scene signal (T4). Fusion remains non-trivial at this data scale, and every task leaves substantial headroom.

Ethics, privacy and limitations

Participants and consent

  • 40 healthy adult volunteers recruited on a university campus, aged 18–26 (mean 21.2); 31 male and 9 female; 26 right-handed and 14 left-handed.
  • Researchers explained the potential risks before each session, including risks from tools and props and a modest loss of hand dexterity from the sensor suite, and obtained written informed consent. Participants who paused or withdrew still received full compensation.
  • All participants signed a media-release agreement for the scene-camera and eye-tracking videos. The full protocol was reviewed and approved by the authors' institutional ethics committee.
  • Participant names, contact details and other directly identifying metadata are kept confidential and are not distributed. Volunteers appear only under pseudonymous IDs (v1–v41).

Use

  • EMG, eye tracking, pressure and first-person video are behavioural and biometric-class signals with privacy and misuse risks. The license forbids re-identification attempts, covert worker surveillance and biometric identification without freely given consent.
  • Models trained on PULSE should not be deployed on new populations without additional validation and ethics review.

Limitations

  • Scale. 40 volunteers, 304 task recordings and 33 motion-primitive recordings suffice for baseline construction but are modest for large-scale activity modeling.
  • Modality coverage. MoCap and pressure are missing from part of the recordings, so hardware-heavy experiments use smaller and potentially less representative subject subsets. The IMU + EMG + eye-tracking core is the most deployment-friendly setting.
  • Optical MoCap. It needs marker placement and lab calibration, and raw absolute joint coordinates are highly subject-dependent. Occlusion, lighting and electrode-placement shifts remain open problems for the wearable subset.
  • Fusion on small data. Multi-modal fusion models overfit more than single-modality backbones; optimization rather than modality count is the main constraint at this scale.
  • Video is auxiliary. Scene-camera video is excluded from the headline benchmark, which targets 100 Hz non-visual signals that 25 fps RGB video cannot capture.
  • Label resolution. Segment boundaries have 1 s resolution and describe semantic intervals, not 100 Hz event times.
  • Open challenges. Several tasks show negative or high-variance results, for example adding IMU to EMG hurts grasp anticipation and pressure prediction remains difficult. The tasks define open problems rather than solved ones.
